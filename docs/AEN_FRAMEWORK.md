# AEN Framework

AEN stands for **Accountability, Effectiveness, and Need**.

It is a three-test rule for evaluating requests for additional public funding and reviewing existing public spending. The framework begins with a burden of proof: before taxpayers are asked for more money, government should demonstrate that existing resources are responsibly managed, current programs work, and additional funding is genuinely necessary.

## A — Accountability

### Core question

Is the money already collected protected, authorized, traceable, and responsibly managed?

### Evidence CivicLens should collect

- Legal authority and stated purpose
- Adopted budget and amendments
- Fund and account classification
- Approving body and vote
- Purchase order, contract, grant, or other obligation
- Vendor and payment records
- Budget-to-actual variance
- Audit findings and management responses
- Internal controls and procurement method
- Change orders and amendments
- Unspent balances, reversions, and transfers

### Findings language

- **Supported:** The records establish the funding source, authorization, spending path, and reconciliation.
- **Partially supported:** Some elements are documented, but important links or reconciliations are missing.
- **Insufficient public evidence:** Available public records do not establish accountability.
- **Exception identified:** An audit, variance, control failure, or unexplained discrepancy requires review.

CivicLens must not label an exception as fraud unless an authoritative finding supports that conclusion.

## E — Effectiveness

### Core question

Did the program, project, or expenditure produce the result it was created to achieve?

### Evidence CivicLens should collect

- Stated objective
- Baseline condition
- Target population or service area
- Performance measure
- Target date and target value
- Actual output and outcome
- Cost per unit or beneficiary when appropriate
- Schedule and completion status
- Comparison with prior years or comparable jurisdictions
- Independent evaluations, audits, inspections, or resident feedback

### Important distinction

Efficiency and effectiveness are related but not identical.

- **Efficiency** asks whether resources were used economically.
- **Effectiveness** asks whether the intended result was achieved.

A program can be inexpensive and ineffective, or costly but effective. CivicLens should display both when data permits, while preserving **Effectiveness** as the E in AEN.

## N — Need

### Core question

Is additional funding truly necessary, or could the problem be solved through reform, reprioritization, management, policy, or existing resources?

### Evidence CivicLens should collect

- Documented problem and scale
- Current demand and capacity
- Legal or safety requirement
- Population and workload changes
- Existing funding and unspent balances
- Alternative solutions considered
- Consequence of no action
- Recurring versus one-time need
- Funding restrictions
- Duplication with other programs or agencies
- Expected result of additional funding

## AEN Review output

Every review should contain:

1. **Question being reviewed**
2. **Scope and time period**
3. **Evidence examined**
4. **Accountability findings**
5. **Effectiveness findings**
6. **Need findings**
7. **Contradictory evidence**
8. **Missing evidence**
9. **Open questions**
10. **Source list and retrieval dates**

## No automatic political verdict

CivicLens may summarize whether each test is supported by the available evidence, but it should not tell users how to vote or claim that an expenditure is inherently good or bad. The platform's job is to make the evidence understandable and auditable.

## Initial Port St. Lucie use case

Example question:

> Before the City increases a tax, fee, or assessment, what do the available records show about the accountability and effectiveness of existing spending, and what evidence demonstrates additional need?
